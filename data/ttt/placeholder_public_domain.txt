Ferrow Hollow lay in a bend of the river where the water slowed and the soil was deep. The families there kept one granary, one weir and one book. The book recorded what each household had brought to the granary and what it had taken out, and it was the only thing in the hollow that everyone agreed to trust.
---
Every spring the book was carried to the long hall and read aloud from the first page to the last. Nobody enjoyed the reading, which took most of a day, but nobody missed it either. A share written in the book was a share, and a share that was not written there did not exist.
---
The rules of the book were simple and old. An entry could be added by the clerk in front of two witnesses. An entry, once added, could not be scratched out. If a mistake was made, a second entry was written to correct it, and both stayed on the page for anyone to see.
---
In the year of the late flood a stranger came to the hollow, lodged with the miller, and studied the rules of the book with great patience. Before the first frost he had found a way, within the letter of every rule, to have a share of the granary written to his name that he had never earned. Then he was gone.
---
The clerk discovered the entry in the week before the reading. She went through the rules three times and could not find one that had been broken. The witnesses had signed. The ink was dry. The share was large enough to carry four families through a bad winter, and the winter was going to be bad.
---
The assembly was called for the following evening. The hall was so full that the doors were left open and people stood in the yard, passing the words back over their shoulders to those who could not hear. The clerk read the entry aloud and then read the rules aloud, and for a while nobody spoke.
---
Two proposals were put. The first was to open the book to the page, strike the entry, and write the ledger forward as if the stranger had never come. The second was to let the book stand exactly as it was written, loss and all, and to change the rules so that such an entry could never be made again.
---
Those who wanted to strike the entry spoke of the four families and the coming winter. The book, they said, existed to serve the hollow and not the other way round. A rule that let a cheat starve honest people was a bad rule, and a page written under a bad rule did not deserve to be kept.
---
Those who wanted the book to stand spoke more quietly. The worth of the book, they said, lay in the fact that nobody could rewrite it, not even the whole hall together, not even for a good reason. Strike one entry for a good reason and the next reason would be a little worse, and the one after that worse again.
---
The vote was taken by standing. Those for striking the entry stood on the side of the hearth, and those for keeping the page stood by the door. The clerk counted twice. The hearth had the greater number, but not by much, and nobody cheered.
---
That night, in the back room of the mill, a dozen people sat up by lamplight and copied the book as it had stood before the vote, page by page, including the stranger's entry. They did it carefully and in silence, and when they were done they had a second book that was the same as the first in every line.
---
In the morning the clerk struck the entry in the hall book and wrote the ledger forward. In the mill the copied book was continued as well, unstruck, with the loss carried in it. From that day the two books received different entries, and the hollow, which had always had one record, now had two.
---
People began to call the two groups by the places they met. The ones who kept the struck book in the long hall were the hall side. The ones who kept the full book in the mill were the mill side. Nobody chose these names, and after a while nobody remembered a time without them.
---
The miller, whose building housed one book and whose grain was counted in the other, took a practical view. He accepted a share written in either book and kept his own tally of both. Many of the traders along the river did the same, though some would take only the hall book and a few would take only the mill book.
---
The children found the whole thing hard to follow. A boy might be told that his bread was counted in the hall book while his cousin's was counted in the mill book, though the loaves came from the same oven. When he asked which book was real, the adults gave different answers, and this taught him more than any single answer would have.
---
In the second month a joint council was called to join the books again. The councillors agreed on everything except the one page. The hall side would not restore the entry and the mill side would not strike it, and because every later page depended on that one, there was no way to make the two ledgers agree.
---
At the harvest the grain was weighed once and written twice. The clerk of the hall wrote it into the hall book and a young man who had sat up by lamplight wrote it into the mill book. The figures were the same to the sack. Only the stranger's share, and everything that followed from it, differed.
---
That autumn a girl from the hall side married a boy from the mill side. Her dowry of grain was entered in both books with both sets of witnesses, and the two clerks stood at the same table to do it. People said afterwards that the wedding had done more to keep the hollow together than any council.
---
The schoolteacher used the matter as a lesson. A record, she told the children, is a story that people have agreed to stop arguing about. The hollow had not stopped arguing, and so it had two stories. Neither story was false. Each was true to the rule that its keepers thought mattered most.
---
Through the winter the mill side grew smaller. Some families returned to the hall book because that was where the traders looked. Some stayed in the mill out of conviction and would have stayed had they been the last. The hall side, for its part, never tried to close the mill or to take its book away.
---
A traveller who stopped at the inn asked which of the two books was the true one. The innkeeper, who kept accounts in both, said that the hall book was true to what the hollow had decided and the mill book was true to what had happened, and that the traveller could pick whichever truth he had come for.
---
At the spring reading the two books were read aloud at the same hour in two places. From the yard of the hall one could hear, faintly, the voice from the mill carrying over the water. For the first hundred pages the two readings were word for word the same, and then, at the one page, they parted.
---
The clerk said later that she had done the only thing the vote allowed and that she had never been sure it was right. She kept a private copy of the struck entry in her desk. It is not clear whether this was out of regret or out of habit, and she did not explain.
---
By the second year the hall book was the book for most purposes. It was the one the traders asked for, the one the new families were entered in, and the one the teacher used for sums. The mill book went on all the same, written up each week by fewer hands, and nobody doubted that it would be written up next week too.
---
The mill side wrote their argument on the inside of the mill door, where everyone who came for flour would see it. It said that a record which can be corrected by a vote is a record of votes, not of events. They did not add anything else, and they did not need to.
---
The hall side put their argument into a rule. From then on the book could be rewritten only if three parts in four of the hollow agreed to it, and only after a full reading of the page in question. They reasoned that a wrong that was plain to almost everyone was a different thing from a wrong that was plain to a bare majority.
---
An old woman who had stood by the door on the night of the vote said that both sides had been right, but about different questions. The hearth had been right about what the hollow owed to four families. The door had been right about what the hollow owed to the book. The mistake, she said, had been to think that one vote could answer both.
---
Below the hollow the river divides around a long island of willows. The two channels run apart for a mile, each with its own current and its own fish, and they meet again below the weir as if they had never been separate. People from the hollow sometimes walk down to look at it, and they do not always say why.
---
Many years later the two books were kept in the same chest in the long hall, side by side, the thicker one and the thinner one. Visitors were shown both. The page where they parted had a ribbon in it, and the ribbon was replaced whenever it wore through.
---
Nobody in the hollow ever decided which book had been the true one, and in time the question stopped seeming important. What they remembered was the shape of the thing: one record, one disputed page, two groups that could not agree on it, and two ledgers that ran on from that page as faithfully as a river runs on from a fork.
